## Substack

**Konu başlığı:** Longevity'nin en popüler ilacı insanlarda ne yaptı?
**Önizleme satırı:** Hayvanlarda kalori kısıtlamasına yakın, insanda ise çok daha mütevazı bir hikaye.

Bu hafta üzerinde en çok durduğum konu rapamisin oldu, çünkü longevity çevrelerinde konuşulan hiçbir molekül bu kadar büyük bir beklenti-gerçeklik makasına sahip değil.

Önce iyi haber: 2025'te Aging Cell'de yayımlanan bir meta-analiz, 167 makaleden 911 etki büyüklüğünü birleştirerek 8 omurgalı türünde (balıktan primatlara) rapamisinin ömür süresine etkisini inceledi. Sonuç, kalori kısıtlamasının güçlü ve tutarlı şekilde ömrü uzattığını, rapamisinin de türler arasında neredeyse aynı tutarlılıkla bunu yaptığını gösterdi. Aynı analizde metformin ise anlamlı bir fayda göstermedi — bu ikisi genelde aynı cümlede anılır ama veri onları aynı kefeye koymuyor.

Şimdi gerçeklik kısmı. İnsanlarda yapılan en büyük, en uzun çalışma PEARL, 48 hafta süren, 50-85 yaş arası sağlıklı yetişkinlerde yapılan, çift kör, plasebo kontrollü bir deneme. Üç grup vardı: plasebo, haftada 5 mg, haftada 10 mg rapamisin. Çalışmanın asıl ölçtüğü şey — visseral (iç organ) yağlanma — hiçbir dozda değişmedi, p değeri 0,942, yani istatistiksel olarak sıfıra yakın bir etki.

Ama tamamen boş da değildi. Kadınlarda 10 mg grubunda yağsız kas kütlesinde artış ve ağrıda azalma, 5 mg grubunda ise öznel iyi oluş hissinde ve genel sağlık algısında iyileşme bulundu. Erkeklerde hiçbir dozda hiçbir fark yoktu. Bu cinsiyete özgü ayrım neden ortaya çıktı bilmiyoruz, ve tek bir çalışmadan genellemek riskli.

Ağustos 2025'te George Washington Üniversitesi'nden bağımsız bir derleme de aynı şeyi söylüyor: hayvanlardaki güçlü sonuçların sağlıklı insanlara aynı ölçüde aktarıldığını gösteren açık bir kanıt yok, daha büyük ve daha iyi tasarlanmış çalışmalara ihtiyaç var.

Benim çıkardığım sonuç: rapamisin hikayesinin en ilginç kısmı belki de şu — bilim bazen "henüz bilmiyoruz" demekten çekinmiyor, ve bu, "kesin işe yarar" demekten daha dürüst bir cevap.

## Medium

**Title:** The Most-Hyped Longevity Drug Just Had Its Biggest Human Test
**Subtitle:** Rapamycin crushes it in animals. The largest human trial tells a much more modest story.

If you've spent any time in longevity circles, you've heard about rapamycin — the immunosuppressant drug, originally isolated from soil on Easter Island, that some researchers now describe as "a pill that mimics caloric restriction." The animal data behind that reputation is genuinely remarkable. The human data, which is finally starting to arrive, tells a much quieter story.

**The animal case: about as good as it gets**

A 2025 meta-analysis in Aging Cell pooled 911 effect sizes from 167 papers across 8 vertebrate species, from fish to primates, comparing rapamycin and metformin against caloric restriction's effect on lifespan. Caloric restriction extended lifespan robustly and consistently. Rapamycin came close behind it, with almost the same level of consistency across species. Metformin — a drug frequently mentioned in the same sentence as rapamycin in longevity discussions — showed no clear benefit at all.

That's about as strong a case as any compound has in the longevity literature. The study's own authors add an important caveat, though: high heterogeneity between studies and publication bias (positive results are more likely to get published) both shape the result, so the true effect may be somewhat smaller than what's reported.

**The human case: a trial that didn't find what it was looking for**

The largest, longest trial testing rapamycin in healthy humans is PEARL, published in the journal Aging in April 2025. It ran 48 weeks, decentralized and double-blind, in adults aged 50 to 85, split across three arms: placebo, 5 mg of rapamycin weekly, or 10 mg weekly.

The trial's primary target was visceral fat. It didn't move, in either dose group, with a p-value of 0.942 — about as close to zero effect as a result gets.

There were secondary findings, but they came with an asterisk. Women in the 10 mg group gained lean tissue mass and reported less pain. Women in the 5 mg group reported better subjective well-being and general health. Men showed no significant change at any dose, on any measure. Why the effect split by sex this cleanly isn't explained by the trial, and it's a single study — not something to build a conclusion on yet.

On safety, the news was reassuring as far as it goes: serious adverse event rates were similar across all three groups, and blood biomarkers stayed within normal ranges throughout the 48 weeks.

**What an independent review makes of all this**

In August 2025, a systematic review by a researcher at George Washington University asked the question directly: is there clinical evidence to support off-label rapamycin use in healthy adults? The answer was no — not yet. The review notes some encouraging signals from trials of related mTOR inhibitors (stronger immune responses, fewer respiratory infections in older adults, improvements in walking speed and strength in a few studies), but closes by calling for larger, better-designed human trials before any of this translates into a recommendation.

**Where that leaves things**

Rapamycin remains the most scientifically interesting compound in longevity research, and that's not nothing. But right now, the gap between what it does in a mouse and what we can show it does in a healthy 60-year-old is wide. The best human trial we have found no effect on its primary target and modest, sex-specific effects on secondary measures. It's also worth remembering that rapamycin used long-term, at high doses, in transplant patients is tied to immunosuppression and metabolic side effects — risks that haven't been ruled out for long-term low-dose use either, simply because no one has studied it that long yet.

None of this makes rapamycin uninteresting. It makes it a drug still waiting for the human trials to catch up to the hype.
