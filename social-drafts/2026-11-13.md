## Substack

**Konu başlığı:** Basınçlı bir odada 90 dakika, hücrelerinizi gerçekten gençleştirir mi?
**Önizleme satırı:** İki küçük İsrail çalışması ilginç bir ipucu veriyor — ama kanıt henüz bu kadar net değil.

Longevity çevrelerinde son zamanlarda sık rastladığım bir iddiayı araştırdım: hiperbarik oksijen tedavisi (HBOT) telomerleri uzatıyor, hücreleri gençleştiriyormuş. Genelde böyle cümlelerin arkasında pazarlama metni çıkar. Bu kez çıkmadı — gerçekten iki yayımlanmış çalışma vardı.

İlki, Tel Aviv Üniversitesi'ne bağlı bir ekibin 2020'de Aging dergisinde yayımladığı çalışma. 64 yaş üstü 35 sağlıklı yetişkine 3 ay boyunca, haftada 5 gün, toplam 60 seans HBOT verilmiş — her seans 90 dakika, %100 oksijen, basınç deniz seviyesinin iki katına çıkarılarak. Sonuç: B hücrelerinde telomer uzunluğu %38 artmış, bu artış istatistiksel olarak anlamlı (p=0,007) ve zaman içindeki analizde de tutarlı kalmış. Yaşlı, artık bölünmeyen "senesan" hücrelerin oranı da düşmüş.

Kulağa etkileyici geliyor, ama bir eksik var: kontrol grubu yok. 35 kişinin hepsi tedaviyi aldı, karşılaştıracak bir plasebo grubu olmadı. Bu da sonucu mevsimsel etkilerden ya da fark edilmeyen yaşam tarzı değişikliklerinden ayırmayı zorlaştırıyor.

İkinci çalışma daha sağlam. Aynı ekip, 63 sağlıklı yetişkini rastgele HBOT (33 kişi) ve kontrol (30 kişi) gruplarına ayırmış. Üç ay sonra HBOT grubunda genel bilişsel skor anlamlı derecede artmış (p=0,0017), en büyük kazanım dikkat ve bilgi işleme hızında. Beyin kan akışını gösteren MR görüntülemesi de bu kazanımı destekliyor.

Peki neden hâlâ temkinliyim? Üç sebep var. Birincisi, her iki çalışma da aynı merkezden geliyor; bağımsız bir ekip tekrarlamadı. İkincisi, örneklemler küçük — 35 ve 63 kişi, kesin sonuç için yeterli değil. Üçüncüsü, ve bence en önemlisi: araştırmacılardan birkaçı, HBOT temelli yaşlanma karşıtı programlar satan bir şirketin kurucu ortağı veya hissedarı. Bu, araştırmayı otomatik olarak geçersiz kılmaz, ama okurken bilmemiz gereken bir bilgi.

HBOT'un güvenlik profili de ilginç: en sık görülen risk kulak barotravması, daha nadir ama ciddi bir risk de oksijen toksisitesine bağlı nöbet (yaklaşık 2000-3000 seansta bir). Yani "zararsız, dene gitsin" türünden bir müdahale de değil.

Sonuç olarak elimde ne var: hücre biyolojisinde gerçek ve ilginç bir sinyal, ama kanıtlanmış bir tedaviden hâlâ uzak. Mekanizma, riskler ve araştırmacıların ticari bağlantıları dahil tüm detaylar yazıda:

## Medium

**Title:** The Oxygen Chamber That Might Be Rewinding Your Cells — Or Might Just Be Good Marketing
**Subtitle:** Two real, peer-reviewed Israeli trials are behind the claim that hyperbaric oxygen therapy lengthens telomeres. Here's what they actually found, and what they don't tell you.

If you've spent any time in longevity forums lately, you've probably seen hyperbaric oxygen therapy described as a telomere-lengthening, cell-rejuvenating treatment. Usually, claims like that trace back to nothing more than a supplement ad. This one is different: there really are two published studies behind it, both from a research group affiliated with Tel Aviv University, both appearing in the journal Aging in 2020.

**The telomere study**

The first trial enrolled 35 healthy, independently living adults aged 64 and older. Thirty completed the full protocol: 60 sessions over roughly three months, five days a week, each session 90 minutes at twice normal atmospheric pressure, breathing 100% oxygen. Blood was drawn at baseline and at several points afterward.

The headline result: telomere length in B cells increased 37.6% by the end of treatment, a change that held up as statistically significant (p=0.007) even when the researchers ran a more conservative analysis across all the time points. Other immune cell types showed increases too, though not all of them held up as cleanly — natural killer cells and T cytotoxic cells showed gains that didn't reach significance in that same stricter analysis. The proportion of senescent cells, the "zombie cells" that stop dividing and secrete inflammatory signals, also dropped meaningfully.

Here's the catch that rarely makes it into the headlines: there was no control group. All 35 participants received the treatment. There was nobody sitting out, nobody on a placebo protocol, nothing to compare against except each person's own starting point. The researchers asked participants not to change their diet, medications, or lifestyle during the study, but a single-arm design like this still can't rule out seasonal effects or small, unnoticed lifestyle shifts. Telomerase activity, the enzyme that would explain how the lengthening actually happened, was never measured.

**The cognitive study**

The second trial is better designed. The same team randomized 63 healthy adults over 64 into an HBOT group (33 people) or a control group (30 people), using the identical protocol. After three months, the HBOT group showed a statistically significant improvement in global cognitive score relative to control (p=0.0017), with the biggest gains in attention and processing speed. Brain imaging showed increased blood flow in frontal and parietal regions in the treatment group, which the authors suggest may explain the cognitive changes.

This one has an actual comparison group, which makes it considerably more convincing than the telomere study. But it's still small, and there was a baseline imbalance worth noting: four people in the HBOT group had a history of atrial fibrillation, versus zero in the control group. We also didn't find this result independently replicated by another lab.

**The mechanism, and the conflict of interest nobody mentions**

The same researchers proposed an explanation in a separate 2020 review: the "hyperoxic-hypoxic paradox." The idea is that cycling between high oxygen and a return to normal oxygen, rather than steady high oxygen, might trick cells into activating some of the same repair pathways normally triggered by low oxygen — stem cell activity, new blood vessel growth, more mitochondria. A protein called HIF-1 sits at the center of this theory. The trouble is that other published research reports HBOT pushing HIF-1 in the opposite direction, so even the proposed mechanism isn't settled.

There's also a detail worth sitting with: several of the authors across these papers are co-founders or shareholders of a company that sells HBOT-based anti-aging programs. That doesn't mean the data is fabricated or the findings are wrong. It does mean the people running the studies have a financial reason to want HBOT to work, which is exactly the kind of thing that should make you want independent replication before updating your beliefs too far.

**What I actually take from this**

HBOT isn't some fringe, unregulated gimmick — it's been an approved treatment for decompression sickness and chronic wounds for decades, and its risk profile is reasonably well understood: ear barotrauma is the most common issue, oxygen-toxicity seizures are rare (roughly 1 in 2,000 to 3,000 sessions), and temporary nearsightedness shows up occasionally. None of that makes it a proven longevity intervention. What we have is a real, interesting signal in cell biology, published by a single group, in small samples, without independent confirmation, from researchers who have skin in the commercial game. That's worth tracking. It isn't worth booking sixty 90-minute sessions over, not yet.
