## Substack

**Konu başlığı:** Balık yağı gerçekten biyolojik yaşınızı mı yavaşlatıyor?
**Önizleme satırı:** 777 kişilik bir çalışma 3-4 ay dedi, 2.692 kişilik başka bir çalışma 2,2 yıl dedi — ama aynı şeyi söylemiyorlar.

Bu hafta omega-3 üzerine iki farklı çalışmayı yan yana koydum, çünkü ikisi de longevity çevrelerinde sık paylaşılıyor ama genelde birbirine karıştırılıyor.

Birincisi İsviçre'deki DO-HEALTH çalışması. 777 kişi üç yıl boyunca günde 1 gram omega-3, 2.000 IU vitamin D ve bir ev egzersiz programına, tek tek veya kombinasyon halinde, randomize edildi. 2025'te Nature Aging'de yayımlanan sonuçlara göre omega-3 tek başına üç farklı epigenetik yaş saatini (PhenoAge, GrimAge2, DunedinPACE) yavaşlattı — üç yılda ortalama 3-4 ay kadar. Vitamin D'nin tek başına anlamlı bir etkisi çıkmadı. Bu küçük bir fark ama randomize bir çalışmadan geliyor, yani nedenselliğe en yakın kanıt türü.

İkincisi çok daha eski: 2013'te Annals of Internal Medicine'da yayımlanan, 2.692 kişiyi 1992'den 2008'e kadar takip eden bir analiz. Kanda en yüksek omega-3 düzeyine sahip beşte birlik grup, en düşük gruba göre ölüm riskinde %27 daha düşük risk taşıyordu ve ortalama 2,2 yıl daha uzun yaşamıştı. Ama bu gözlemsel bir çalışma — yüksek omega-3 düzeyine sahip insanlar muhtemelen daha düzenli balık yiyor, farklı bir genel yaşam tarzına sahip olabilir. Nedenselliği kanıtlamıyor.

Bu ikisini birbirine karıştırmamak önemli. Biri "omega-3 verirsen ne olur" sorusuna cevap veriyor (küçük ama gerçek bir etki), diğeri "omega-3 düzeyi yüksek olanlar kim" sorusuna (büyük bir ilişki, ama nedenini tam bilmiyoruz). İkisini tek bir "omega-3 mucizedir" cümlesinde eritmek, verinin söylediğinden fazlasını iddia etmek oluyor.

## Medium

**Title:** Two Omega-3 Studies Everyone Cites — and Why They Don't Say the Same Thing
**Subtitle:** A randomized trial found a 3-4 month slowdown in biological age. An older cohort found a 2.2-year survival gap. Neither means what the headlines imply.

Omega-3 has been "good for your heart" for so long that it barely registers as news anymore. But two studies, cited constantly in longevity circles, push the claim further — into biological age itself — and they rarely get compared properly.

**The randomized trial: a small, real effect**

The DO-HEALTH trial, run in Switzerland, followed 777 people for three years across eight treatment arms: vitamin D (2,000 IU/day), omega-3 (1 gram EPA+DHA/day), and a home exercise program, alone or combined. Published in 2025 in Nature Aging, the results showed that omega-3 alone slowed three separate epigenetic age clocks — PhenoAge, GrimAge2, and DunedinPACE. The size of the effect, translated into time, worked out to an average of 3 to 4 months of slowing over the three-year study. Vitamin D alone showed no significant effect on any clock. When all three interventions were combined, there was an additional benefit on the PhenoAge clock specifically.

That's a modest number. But it comes from a randomized trial, which means it gets closer to showing cause and effect than almost anything else in this space.

**The cohort study: a bigger number, a bigger asterisk**

The second study is older and much larger in a different sense. Published in 2013 in Annals of Internal Medicine, it followed 2,692 American adults, averaging 74 years old, from 1992 to 2008. Blood levels of omega-3 fatty acids were measured once, at the start. People in the highest fifth for total omega-3 had a 27% lower risk of death from any cause compared to the lowest fifth (hazard ratio 0.73). Press coverage of the study put the life-expectancy gap at about 2.2 years.

This is a far bigger apparent effect than DO-HEALTH's few months. But it's an observational study. Nobody was assigned to have high or low omega-3 — the researchers just measured where people naturally fell and tracked what happened to them. People with naturally high omega-3 levels likely eat more fish, which often comes bundled with other differences in diet, income, or healthcare access. The study's authors tried to adjust for known confounders, but an observational design can never fully rule this out. A separate 2021 analysis of the Framingham Offspring cohort found a similar pattern — omega-3 index among the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality out of 28 fatty-acid measures — but we're deliberately not repeating the "as powerful as smoking" comparison that circulated in press coverage of that paper, since that framing doesn't appear in the study itself.

**Why the mismatch matters**

These two studies answer different questions. DO-HEALTH asks: if you give someone omega-3, what happens to a lab measure of their biological age? The cohort studies ask: do people who happen to have high omega-3 in their blood live longer? The second question has a much bigger observed effect size, but a much weaker claim to causation.

Collapsing both into "omega-3 extends life" erases that distinction. The honest version is messier: a randomized trial shows a small, real, short-term effect on biological aging; a large observational link exists between blood omega-3 and longevity, and it probably reflects both the fatty acids themselves and the broader diet and lifestyle pattern that comes with eating more fish.

**What to actually do with this**

Two servings a week of oily fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel) is the standard, well-supported way to raise your blood omega-3 level. If you're considering a supplement, DO-HEALTH used 1 gram of EPA+DHA daily. Anyone on blood thinners, awaiting surgery, or considering a dose above 2-3 grams a day should talk to a doctor — high-dose omega-3 can raise bleeding risk.
