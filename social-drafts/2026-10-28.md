## Substack

**Konu başlığı:** Uyku hakkında "bilinen" şeylerin çoğu kaynaksız — işte rakamlarla üç çalışma
**Önizleme satırı:** 9 saat uyumak kulağa iyi geliyor ama 2025 meta-analizinde en yüksek risk orada çıktı.

Bu hafta uyku konusuna girdim, çünkü longevity içeriğinde garip bir boşluk fark ettim: herkes "uyku önemli" diyor ama kimse rakam vermiyor. Üç farklı çalışmayı art arda okuyunca ortaya üç farklı mesaj çıktı, ben de hepsini tek yazıda topladım.

İlki süreyle ilgili. Ungvari ve arkadaşlarının 2025'te GeroScience'ta yayımladığı meta-analiz, 79 kohort çalışmasını birleştirmiş. 7-8 saati referans alınca, 7 saatten az uyuyanlarda ölüm riski %14 daha yüksek, ama 9 saat ve üzeri uyuyanlarda %34 daha yüksek. Yani "bol uyku her zaman iyidir" varsayımı burada çökmüş durumda — tabii bunun bir kısmı, uzun uykunun zaten bir hastalığın belirtisi olabilmesinden kaynaklanıyor olabilir.

İkincisi düzenle ilgili, ve bana göre yazının en ilginç kısmı. 2023'te eLife'ta yayımlanan bir çalışma, UK Biobank'tan 88.975 kişinin bilek sensörü verisiyle bir "uyku düzenlilik puanı" hesaplamış — her gece aynı saatte uyuyup kalkıyor musunuz, yoksa saatleriniz rastgele mi? En düzensiz %5'lik dilimde ölüm riski ortanca gruba kıyasla %53 daha yüksek çıkmış; en düzenli %5'lik dilimde ise %10 daha düşük. Yani belki de "kaç saat" sorusundan önce "hep aynı saatte mi" sorusunu sormak gerekiyor.

Üçüncüsü ise beni en çok şaşırtan taraf: 2016'da Women's Health Initiative verisiyle yapılan bir çalışmada, 2.078 kadının kendi bildirdiği uyku süresi (kısa ya da uzun olması) epigenetik yaşla hiç ilişkili çıkmamış. Ama uykuya dalmakta zorlanma, gece uyanma gibi uykusuzluk belirtileri ilişkiliymiş — beş belirtinin tamamını taşıyan kadınlar, hiç belirtisi olmayan yaşıtlarına göre biyolojik olarak ortalama iki yaş daha yaşlı görünüyormuş.

Hepsi gözlemsel veri, hiçbiri "uykunu değiştir, ömrün uzasın" demiyor. Ama üçü birlikte okununca pratik bir çerçeve çıkıyor: saatleri sabitlemek, aşırı uzun uykuyu sorgulamak ve "kaç saat" sayacından çok uykusuzluk belirtilerine bakmak.

## Medium

**Title:** Three Sleep Studies That Don't Agree With Each Other — And That's the Interesting Part
**Subtitle:** Duration, regularity and insomnia symptoms each track mortality and biological age differently. Treating them as one thing misses what the data actually shows.

Most advice about sleep and longevity collapses into one sentence: sleep more, it's good for you. Three separate lines of research, read together, complicate that sentence in useful ways.

**Sleeping too much carries more risk than sleeping too little**

A 2025 meta-analysis by Ungvari and colleagues in GeroScience pooled 79 cohort studies with at least a year of follow-up. Using 7-8 hours as the reference, short sleep (under 7 hours) was linked to 14% higher all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 1.14, 95% CI 1.10-1.18). Long sleep (9 hours or more) was linked to 34% higher mortality (hazard ratio 1.34, 95% CI 1.26-1.42) — and the effect was stronger in women. The long-sleep side of the curve carried the bigger number, which cuts against the instinct that more sleep is simply better. It's worth noting this doesn't prove long sleep causes harm; conditions like depression or untreated sleep apnea can cause someone to sleep longer while also raising their mortality risk independently.

**Your schedule might matter more than your total hours**

A 2023 study in eLife, led by Cribb and colleagues, asked a sharper question: holding duration aside, does waking and sleeping at consistent times matter on its own? Using 7 days of wrist-accelerometer data from 88,975 UK Biobank participants, the researchers built a Sleep Regularity Index from 0 to 100. Over a mean follow-up of 7.1 years, with 3,010 deaths recorded, people in the most irregular 5th percentile (SRI around 41) had 53% higher mortality than those at the median, while people in the most regular 95th percentile (SRI around 75) had 10% lower mortality. Cardiovascular and cancer deaths showed a similar pattern. It's a genuinely striking result, though irregular sleepers often also sleep less and more poorly overall, so isolating regularity's independent contribution is harder than the headline number suggests.

**What tracks with biological age isn't hours — it's insomnia**

The clearest nuance comes from a 2016 Biological Psychiatry study using Women's Health Initiative data on 2,078 women averaging 64.5 years old. Self-reported short or long sleep duration had no relationship with epigenetic age. But insomnia symptoms — trouble falling asleep, waking during the night, early waking without getting back to sleep — were linked to an older epigenetic age even after adjusting for other factors (B=1.02, SE=0.37, p=0.005). UCLA's coverage of the study noted that postmenopausal women with all five insomnia symptoms looked roughly two years biologically older than peers with none. A 2024 Clinical Epigenetics study of 692 Korean adults found a related pattern: the link between sleep quality scores and epigenetic age acceleration only reached significance within the subgroup of poor sleepers, not across the whole sample.

**What this doesn't mean**

None of these are randomized trials. None shows that changing how long, how regularly, or how well you sleep will extend your life or protect your cognition — they show associations in specific cohorts, mostly Western and middle-to-upper socioeconomic populations. The Framingham study on deep sleep and dementia risk (27% higher dementia risk per percentage-point annual decline in slow-wave sleep) had only 52 dementia cases in 346 participants, a small number that limits how much weight the finding can carry.

Put together, though, these three studies suggest a more specific set of questions than "am I getting enough sleep": Are you sleeping dramatically more than 8 hours, and if so, why? Do your bed and wake times stay roughly consistent across the week? And separate from the hour count — do you actually fall asleep easily and stay asleep? The data suggests that last question might be the one actually worth tracking.
