## Substack

**Konu başlığı:** BMI'nız normal, ama kalbiniz sizden daha yaşlı olabilir
**Önizleme satırı:** Üç farklı araştırma ekibi, üç farklı organda, aynı şüpheliyi buldu: karnın içindeki görünmeyen yağ.

Bu hafta üzerinde durduğum konu biraz rahatsız edici, çünkü "kilonuz normalse sorun yok" varsayımını zorluyor.

Mesele visseral yağ — deri altında elle tutabildiğiniz yağdan farklı olarak, karın boşluğunda karaciğer, pankreas ve bağırsakların arasına yerleşen yağ dokusu. Dışarıdan bakınca fark etmeyebilirsiniz, ama bu doku metabolik olarak oldukça hareketli: iltihap sinyali veren proteinler salgılıyor, kan şekeri düzenlenmesine doğrudan karışıyor.

2025-2026'da yayımlanan üç ayrı çalışma bu dokuyu üç farklı organ sisteminde test etti. Imperial College London'ın İngiltere Biobank verisiyle yaptığı analiz, 21.241 kişide yapay zekayla hesaplanan "kalp yaşının", visseral yağı fazla olanlarda takvim yaşının önüne geçtiğini buldu — ilginç olan, BMI'nin bu konuda zayıf bir gösterge çıkması. Zayıf görünen biri de fazla visseral yağa sahip olabiliyor.

Avustralya'dan Batı Avustralya Üniversitesi ekibi, 1966'dan beri sürdürülen bir kohortta yaklaşık 4.800 kişiyi inceledi ve visseral yağı fazla olanlarda biyolojik yaşlanmanın hızlandığını, kadınlarda ayrıca telomer kısalmasıyla bağlantılı olduğunu buldu. Bu ilişki BMI ve bel çevresi zaten hesaba katıldıktan sonra da duruyordu.

En ilginç veri ise İsrail'den Ben Gurion Üniversitesi ve Harvard'ın birlikte yürüttüğü uzun dönem takip çalışmasından geldi. Eski diyet deneylerine katılmış kişiler yıllar sonra tekrar çağrılmış; 188 kişilik bir alt grupta beyin MR'ı üç kez tekrarlanmış. Sonuç: kilo geri alınsa bile, visseral yağdaki kalıcı azalma hem daha yavaş beyin atrofisiyle hem daha düşük tip 2 diyabet riskiyle ilişkili kalıyor. Deri altı yağda aynı etki yok.

Hiçbiri "visseral yağı azaltırsanız yaşlanmayı durdurursunuz" demiyor — ikisi tamamen gözlemsel, üçüncüsü de randomize bir deney değil. Ama üçü birlikte okunduğunda, kilonun kendisinden çok nerede biriktiğinin önemli olduğu fikri oldukça güçleniyor.

## Medium

**Title:** Your BMI Looks Fine. Your Heart Might Not Agree.
**Subtitle:** Three separate 2025-2026 studies point to the same culprit behind faster aging in the heart, brain and cells — and it's not how much you weigh.

There's a specific kind of body fat that doesn't show up on a scale, doesn't always show up in the mirror, and according to three independent research teams, seems to age the heart, the brain and the cells faster than almost anything else researchers have measured.

**The fat that isn't where you think**

Most people picture body fat as the kind under the skin, soft tissue you can pinch at the hip or the arm. Visceral fat is different. It sits deep inside the abdominal cavity, wrapped around the liver, pancreas and intestines. You can have a flat stomach and still carry a meaningful amount of it. It also behaves differently from subcutaneous fat: it's metabolically active, secreting inflammatory signaling proteins and interfering with blood sugar regulation in ways ordinary fat doesn't.

**What a 21,241-person heart study found**

A team led by Declan O'Regan at Imperial College London and the MRC Laboratory of Medical Sciences used UK Biobank imaging data from 21,241 people, feeding 126 heart-image traits into an algorithm that estimated each person's "heart age." Published in the European Heart Journal in August 2025, the results showed visceral fat was one of the strongest predictors of a heart age running ahead of actual age, in both men and women, and tied to inflammatory markers in blood tests. BMI, notably, performed poorly as a predictor. The researchers also found a sex split: an "apple-shaped" fat pattern predicted faster heart aging in men, while a genetic tendency toward hip-and-thigh fat in women tracked with the opposite, slower effect.

**A second study, a different organ system, the same pattern**

Researchers at the University of Western Australia turned to the long-running Busselton Healthy Ageing Study, comparing DXA-measured visceral fat in roughly 4,800 people aged 45 to 69 against markers of biological age acceleration and telomere length. Published in Obesity in 2026, the study found that more visceral fat tracked with faster biological aging in both sexes, and in women specifically, with shorter telomeres, even after adjusting for overall body fat, BMI, waist size and lifestyle habits.

**The data that comes closest to proving cause and effect**

The most compelling piece comes from a long-term follow-up led by Iris Shai of Ben Gurion University, run jointly with Harvard's T.H. Chan School of Public Health. Participants from older diet trials were brought back years later; a subset of 188 had repeat brain MRIs at baseline, 18 months and 5 years, alongside cognitive testing. People who kept their cumulative visceral fat lower over that period showed slower expansion of the brain's ventricles, a recognized marker of tissue loss, and scored better on cognitive tests, published in Nature Communications in March 2026. Crucially, this effect held up even in people who regained the weight they'd lost. Subcutaneous fat showed none of it. A related analysis in Circulation, published in June 2026, found that each 10% reduction in visceral fat achieved in the original trial was independently associated with roughly a 30% lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes five to ten years later, regardless of whether weight came back.

**What this doesn't prove**

Two of these three studies are observational snapshots; they describe a relationship but can't rule out other explanations entirely. The third comes closer to a causal story because it starts from a controlled intervention, but it's still a long-term re-analysis of older trials, not a new randomized experiment. The three teams also measured visceral fat with different tools, MRI, DXA, repeat MRI, each with its own precision. None of this is a reason to take the findings lightly, but it is a reason not to oversell them.

**What actually changes anything**

Visceral fat responds to exercise, particularly a mix of aerobic activity and resistance training, more reliably than subcutaneous fat does, which makes it a more specific target than weight loss in general. Waist circumference, while not a perfect stand-in, is a far more useful rough signal than BMI. The honest summary of where this research stands: where the fat sits appears to matter more than how much of it there is, and that's a genuinely new way to think about the number on the scale.
