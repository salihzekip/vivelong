Not All Fat Does the Same Job

Not all body fat behaves the same way. The fat under your skin, the kind you can pinch at the hip, thigh or arm, works mostly as a passive energy store. Visceral fat sits somewhere else entirely: inside the abdominal cavity, around and between the liver, pancreas and intestines. Someone whose waist doesn't look large from the outside can still carry a lot of it, which is one reason waist circumference alone isn't always a reliable signal.

The real difference is in how this tissue behaves. It is metabolically active, it secretes inflammatory signaling proteins, it sits close to the liver's blood supply, and it is tangled up in the processes that drive insulin resistance. Longevity researchers have taken a sharper interest in it over the past year or two because newer imaging methods can now separate it from BMI or waist size and measure it directly. The result is that three independent research teams, looking at three different organ systems, landed on a strikingly similar pattern.

Heart Age: A 21,241-Person UK Biobank Analysis

A study led by Declan O'Regan of Imperial College London and the MRC Laboratory of Medical Sciences, published in the European Heart Journal in August 2025, used data from 21,241 people in the UK Biobank who had undergone heart and vascular imaging. The team used AI to turn 126 imaging-derived heart traits into a "heart age" for each person, then compared it against their actual age.

  • Visceral fat was one of the strongest predictors: In both sexes, people with more fat inside the abdomen had heart ages running ahead of their chronological age, and the pattern lined up with inflammatory markers found in blood samples.
  • BMI fell short: The researchers reported that BMI was a poor predictor of heart age, and that where fat sits mattered more than how much of it there was overall.
  • A sex difference: In men, an "apple-shaped" distribution concentrated around the abdomen tracked with faster heart aging. In women, a genetic tendency toward hip-and-thigh ("pear-shaped") fat pulled heart age the other way, toward a slower trajectory, and higher estrogen levels in premenopausal women were linked to slower heart aging too.

The study is observational, and the authors say so plainly. But the practical takeaway is that someone who looks lean, with a BMI inside the normal range, can still carry a lot of visceral fat, and their heart age can run ahead of their actual age. "My weight is normal, so I'm not at risk" doesn't hold up against this data.

Cellular Aging: An Australian Cohort

A study from Riordan O'Shea and colleagues at the University of Western Australia, published in Obesity in 2026, drew on the Busselton Healthy Ageing Study, which has run since 1966. The team compared DXA-measured visceral fat in roughly 4,800 people aged 45 to 69 (2,614 of them women) against "phenotypic age acceleration," a biological-aging marker, and leukocyte telomere length.

  • More visceral fat, faster biological aging: This showed up in both men and women, and it held after accounting for overall body fat, BMI, waist circumference and lifestyle factors.
  • A telomere link in women: In women, higher visceral fat also tracked with shorter telomere length. Our telomeres article covers in more detail what these DNA end-caps have to do with cellular aging.
  • A proposed mechanism: The researchers suggest the inflammatory proteins visceral fat secretes may drive systemic inflammation and metabolic stress, which could explain the pattern.

This, too, is an observational study and doesn't prove cause and effect. But because BMI and waist circumference were already accounted for, it's harder to wave the finding away as simply "they were heavier to begin with."

The Brain: An Effect That Outlasts Weight Regain

The most interesting data comes from long-term follow-up work led by Iris Shai of Ben Gurion University, announced jointly with the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health. An analysis published in Nature Communications in March 2026 recalled participants from earlier diet and lifestyle trials years later; in one subset, 188 participants had repeat brain MRIs at baseline, 18 months and 5 years, along with cognitive testing using the MoCA.

  • Less visceral fat, slower brain atrophy: Participants with lower cumulative visceral fat over the follow-up period showed slower expansion of the brain's ventricles, a well-established marker of tissue loss, and scored better on cognitive tests.
  • The effect held even with weight regain: This relationship showed up even in people who had lost weight and later regained it; the researchers attribute much of the effect to blood sugar regulation (glycemic control).
  • No such link for subcutaneous fat: Neither superficial nor deep subcutaneous fat showed a meaningful connection to the brain measurements, which points to something specific to visceral fat rather than body weight in general.

A separate analysis from the same group, published in Circulation in June 2026, looked at the cardiometabolic side: each 10% reduction in visceral fat achieved during the original diet trial was independently associated with roughly a 30% lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes 5 to 10 years later, a relationship that held up after accounting for weight change, diet adherence and physical activity levels. That's closer to causal evidence than the other two studies in this set, since it starts from a controlled intervention. It is still not a randomized controlled trial, though, but a long-term observational follow-up of earlier experiments.

So What Should You Do?

All three studies converge on the same point: visceral fat carries a risk separate from total weight or BMI, and reducing it specifically is a more targeted goal than losing weight in general. The good news is that visceral fat is known to respond to exercise more readily than subcutaneous fat; a combination of regular aerobic activity and resistance training has the most consistent evidence behind it. Our strength training article covers this in more depth.

Earlier research had already shown that GLP-1 drugs reduce visceral fat; the Imperial team notes that these drugs' effect on heart aging specifically still needs to be studied, but that is a proposed direction for future research, not a finding that already exists. Our GLP-1 drugs and longevity article looks in detail at what these drugs have and haven't shown so far.

On the practical side, there's no way to measure visceral fat directly at home; it takes imaging such as DXA or MRI. Waist circumference isn't a perfect substitute, but it's a more useful rough proxy than BMI alone. We covered how organ-specific aging is measured more generally in our organ age article; heart age is one piece of that same framework.

Limitations

  • Two of the three studies are purely observational: The UK Biobank and Australian cohort both capture a relationship at a single point in time; they don't fully answer who accumulates visceral fat, or why.
  • Causation still isn't proven: The Harvard-Ben Gurion follow-up gives a stronger signal because it starts from a controlled intervention, but it's still not a randomized controlled trial, just a long-term re-examination of older experiments.
  • The measurement methods differ: The heart-age study used MRI, the Australian study used DXA, and the brain study used repeat MRI; the three methods capture visceral fat with different precision.
  • The 188-person brain subgroup is small: It's a small slice of the larger cohort, so the finding needs confirmation in bigger samples.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is visceral fat, and how is it different from subcutaneous fat?

Visceral fat sits deep inside the abdominal cavity, around and between organs such as the liver, pancreas and intestines, unlike subcutaneous fat, which you can pinch under the skin. It is metabolically active: it secretes inflammatory signaling proteins and directly affects blood sugar regulation. Studies published in 2025-2026 found no link between subcutaneous fat and biological aging, while visceral fat was consistently linked to markers of heart, brain and cellular aging.

Does visceral fat cause faster biological aging, or is it just correlated?

Most of the evidence is observational: people with less visceral fat tend to have better heart, brain and cell-aging markers, but that alone doesn't prove cause and effect. The exception is a long-term follow-up study from Ben Gurion University and Harvard, which found that even when weight was regained, a sustained reduction in visceral fat remained linked to slower brain atrophy and lower type 2 diabetes risk. That is closer to causal evidence than a single observational snapshot, but it is still not a randomized trial.

Does reducing visceral fat actually slow aging?

It's too early to say for certain. But the strongest evidence available shows that a sustained reduction in visceral fat, even when weight is later regained, is independently linked to lower type 2 diabetes risk and slower brain atrophy. Exercise, especially a combination of aerobic and resistance training, has the most consistent evidence for reducing visceral fat specifically; waist circumference, while imperfect, is a more useful proxy than BMI alone.
