The Short Answer

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) has picked up a reputation in longevity circles as an intervention that "lengthens telomeres and rejuvenates cells." That reputation has a real source: a team affiliated with Tel Aviv University published two studies in Aging in 2020, one measuring telomere length and senescent cells, the other testing cognitive performance. Both are genuine, peer-reviewed, published papers.

But both are small, one lacks a control group entirely, and some of the researchers hold a financial stake in a company that sells HBOT-based products. What we actually have is an interesting, promising early signal — not the level of proof that "reversed aging" headlines imply.

What Is HBOT, and How Is It Done?

HBOT means entering a pressurized chamber or capsule and breathing 100% oxygen at a pressure higher than normal atmospheric pressure. Standard protocols raise the pressure to twice sea level (2 ATA); this higher pressure lets oxygen dissolve directly into blood plasma and other body fluids, not just bind to hemoglobin in red blood cells. The result is more oxygen reaching tissue than normal breathing could ever deliver.

The treatment has been an approved medical use for decades, for decompression sickness, carbon monoxide poisoning, chronic non-healing wounds, and radiation injury, among others. In recent years, a handful of research centers have started testing the same tool against aging-related cellular changes — the two studies at the center of this article both come from that line of research.

The Telomere and Senescence Study: Hachmo et al., 2020

The study, run by the Sagol Center for Hyperbaric Medicine and Research at Shamir Medical Center, enrolled 35 healthy, independently living adults aged 64 and older. Thirty completed the protocol; telomere length was analyzed in the 25 participants with sufficient blood samples, and senescent cell proportion in 20. The protocol ran five days a week for about three months, 60 sessions in total; each session lasted 90 minutes at 2 ATA on 100% oxygen, with a 5-minute air break every 20 minutes to reduce the risk of oxygen toxicity.

Blood was drawn at baseline, at the 30th and 60th sessions, and 1-2 weeks after the final session. Telomere length was measured in four immune cell types:

  • B cells: a 37.6% increase at the final measurement versus baseline (p=0.007), which also remained significant in the repeated-measures analysis over time (p=0.017) — the only result among the four cell types that stayed significant across that analysis.
  • T helper cells: a 29.3% increase (p=0.005), though this stayed only borderline in the repeated-measures analysis (p=0.063).
  • Natural killer cells: a 22.2% increase, right at the edge of significance at the final measurement (p=0.06).
  • T cytotoxic cells: a 19.6% increase (p=0.023), which did not reach significance in the repeated-measures analysis.

The proportion of senescent cells — cells that have stopped dividing and secrete inflammatory signals — also fell: senescent T helper cells dropped 37.3% (p<0.0001), and senescent T cytotoxic cells dropped 11% (p=0.0004). The authors described this as a possible "senolytic effect," proposing a mechanism alternative to the drug-based approaches we cover in our senolytics article.

The study's biggest limitation is the absence of a control group. All 35 participants received HBOT; there was no placebo or waitlist arm for comparison. Although the authors asked participants not to change their diet, medication, or lifestyle, this single-arm design makes it hard to rule out seasonal effects or unnoticed lifestyle changes. Telomerase enzyme activity was also never measured, so the biological pathway behind the lengthening remains unclear.

The Cognitive RCT: Hadanny et al., 2020

The same team's second paper, published the same year in Aging, has a sturdier design: 63 healthy adults over 64 were randomly assigned to either HBOT (33 people) or a control arm (30 people). The protocol matched the first study — 60 sessions, 100% oxygen, 2 ATA, 90 minutes per session, over three months.

After three months, the difference between the HBOT and control groups in global cognitive score over time was statistically significant (p=0.0017). The largest gains were in attention and information processing speed, with effect sizes of 0.745 and 0.788 respectively — values considered medium-to-large in psychological research. Perfusion MRI showed significantly increased cerebral blood flow in the HBOT group, in several frontal and parietal regions, compared to controls; the authors suggested the cognitive gains might be connected to these regional blood flow changes.

This study can reasonably be considered more reliable than the first, since it includes a control arm. But the sample is still small, and the groups had a baseline imbalance: 4 people (13.3%) in the HBOT group had a history of atrial fibrillation, versus none in the control group. Aside from that, the groups were demographically similar. We did not find this result replicated by an independent research team.

The Mechanism: The Hyperoxic-Hypoxic Paradox

A 2020 review published in Biomolecules, by the same research group, tries to explain HBOT's effect through a concept called the "hyperoxic-hypoxic paradox" (HHP). The idea: cells aren't exposed to constant high oxygen, but to repeated cycles of high oxygen followed by a return to normal oxygen. According to the authors, this cycling can trigger some of the cellular responses the body normally mounts under low oxygen (hypoxia) — stem cell activation, increased mitochondrial production, new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis). A protein called HIF-1, which lets cells sense low oxygen, sits at the center of the proposed mechanism.

This model is still disputed. The review itself notes that other published work has reported hyperbaric oxygen lowering HIF-1 under some conditions — so there is no consensus on which direction the effect actually runs. There is also a common thread running through this review and the two clinical studies above: several of the authors are co-founders or shareholders of AVIV Scientific, a company that sells HBOT-based anti-aging programs. That doesn't automatically invalidate the research, but it is a conflict of interest worth keeping in mind while reading it.

Unknowns and Limitations

  • No independent replication: Both studies come from the same Israeli center; we did not find a different team testing the same protocol in a different population.
  • No long-term follow-up: Participants were measured again only 1-2 weeks after the final session. Whether the telomere increase holds up months or years later is unknown.
  • Mechanism unresolved: Telomerase enzyme activity was never measured, so the biological pathway behind the lengthening is unclear.
  • Cost and time burden: The protocol runs five days a week for three months, 90 minutes per session — a significant commitment of both money and time.
  • Commercial ties: Some of the researchers have a direct financial relationship with a company selling HBOT-based products.

Risks: Is HBOT Safe?

HBOT has been used for decades for decompression sickness and chronic wound care and is generally considered safe — but it is not risk-free.

  • Middle-ear barotrauma: The most common side effect; some sources put it at roughly 2% of treatments. It is largely preventable with equalization techniques or ear tubes.
  • Seizure from oxygen toxicity: The most serious neurological risk, reported at a rate of roughly 1 in 2,000 to 3,000 sessions. A seizure generally stops once oxygen is withdrawn and does not by itself mean the person has epilepsy.
  • Temporary myopia: Blurred distance vision; a common but generally reversible effect.
  • Pulmonary oxygen toxicity: Rare under standard protocols.
  • Drug interactions and contraindications: Certain drugs, such as disulfiram, and conditions like untreated pneumothorax are incompatible with HBOT; a physician evaluation is required before starting treatment.

What This Means in Practice

The data we have suggests HBOT may positively affect some cellular markers linked to aging — but presenting that as a "proven anti-aging treatment" goes beyond what the current evidence supports. The studies are small, one has no control group, and both come from the same center, with researchers partly tied to the same company. At the same time, the findings are real, published in peer-reviewed journals, and interesting enough to merit independent replication.

Starting HBOT today purely to lower your biological age means assigning more weight than the evidence supports to an intervention that demands hours of time, real money, and rare but genuine risks. Reaching for HBOT before prioritizing far better-supported areas like sleep and strength training would be putting resources in the wrong place. Being curious about it is reasonable; deciding without talking to a doctor, and without calibrating expectations to the real size of these studies, is not.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does HBOT really lengthen telomeres?

A 2020 Israeli study published in Aging gave 60 sessions of hyperbaric oxygen therapy to 35 adults over 64 and found increased telomere length in some immune cell types afterward; B cells showed a 37.6% increase (p=0.007) that also held up in the repeated-measures analysis over time. But the study had no control group, the sample was small, and telomerase activity was never measured. The finding is real, but it does not yet count as definitive proof.

Is HBOT's cognitive benefit proven?

The same team published a randomized controlled trial in which 63 healthy adults over 64 were assigned to HBOT (33 people) or a control arm (30 people). After three months, the difference in global cognitive score between groups over time was statistically significant (p=0.0017), with the largest gains in attention and information processing speed. This is a sturdier study because it has a control arm, but the sample is still small and the groups had a baseline imbalance in atrial fibrillation history.

What is the hyperoxic-hypoxic paradox?

A model proposed by the same research group: when cells are exposed not to constant high oxygen but to repeated cycles of high oxygen followed by a return to normal oxygen, this cycling may trigger some of the same cellular responses the body normally produces under low oxygen (hypoxia) — stem cell activation, new blood vessel formation, increased mitochondrial production. A protein called HIF-1, which senses low oxygen, sits at the center of the proposed mechanism. But the literature disagrees on which direction HBOT actually pushes this protein, so the mechanism is not settled.

What are the known risks of HBOT?

The most common side effect is middle-ear barotrauma, reported in roughly 2% of treatments in some sources, and usually preventable. A rarer but more serious risk is a seizure from oxygen toxicity, reported at a rate of roughly 1 in 2,000 to 3,000 treatment sessions. Temporary, usually reversible nearsightedness is another common effect. Because HBOT can interact with certain medications and conditions, a physician evaluation is needed before starting treatment.

Should I start HBOT to lower my biological age?

Current evidence is not enough to recommend HBOT as a proven anti-aging treatment. The studies are small, one has no control group, and some of the researchers have a financial relationship with a company that sells HBOT-based products. Prioritizing areas with much stronger evidence, such as exercise, sleep, and nutrition, while discussing HBOT with a physician and calibrating expectations to the real size of these studies, is the more reasonable approach.
