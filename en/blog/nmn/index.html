Short Answer

NMN is an intermediate molecule cells use to make NAD+. It became a fixture of longevity research after mouse studies showed it slowed age-related physical decline. In humans the picture is far more modest: one trial found it raised muscle insulin sensitivity, another found it improved walking speed and grip strength in one hand, and several other measures, including blood sugar, blood pressure, cholesterol and body composition, showed no change at all. The most widely shared telomere claim rests on an uncontrolled pilot study in just 8 people.

NMN supplement searches are growing fast, and most of what circulates about it online is written by companies selling it. Here we go to the actual trials instead: who ran them, with how many people, at what dose, and what they found.

What Is NMN, and How Does It Relate to NAD+?

NMN (nicotinamide mononucleotide) is a direct precursor the body converts into NAD+, a coenzyme required for cellular energy production and DNA repair enzymes, and one whose levels fall sharply with age. We cover NAD+ itself, its link to sirtuin genes, and other supplements in that space (resveratrol, NR) in more depth in our NAD+ and sirtuin genes article; here we focus specifically on NMN's human trials, a part that usually gets compressed into a single sentence elsewhere.

Whether oral NMN survives digestion intact was long debated. Mouse studies showed it converts to NAD+ in tissue quickly, and several human trials confirmed that blood NAD+ rises with supplementation. What remains contested is what that rise actually translates into.

The Mouse Data: Where the Interest Started

Shin-ichiro Imai's team at Washington University gave regular chow-fed mice NMN in their drinking water from age 5 months to 17 months, 12 months in total, in a study published in Cell Metabolism in 2016. There were three groups: high dose, low dose, and an untreated control. The study didn't measure how long the mice lived; it tracked markers of age-related physiological decline.

  • Metabolism: NMN suppressed age-related weight gain, improved energy metabolism and insulin sensitivity, and improved the plasma lipid profile.
  • Movement: Physical activity increased, and mitochondrial function in muscle tissue improved.
  • Eyes: Markers of age-related decline in eye function improved.
  • Age mattered: The effects showed up only in older mice. Young mice saw no visible benefit, because their tissues already make enough NMN on their own.

This is the reference study that pushed NMN into human research. But it is worth being precise about what it measured: markers of decline, not mouse lifespan. "NMN extended mice's lifespan" does not follow from this study.

Human Data 1: Muscle Insulin Sensitivity

A team led by Samuel Klein and Shin-ichiro Imai at Washington University randomized 25 prediabetic, overweight or obese postmenopausal women to 250 mg of NMN a day or placebo for 10 weeks, in a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in Science in 2021. Of the 25 who completed it, 13 were in the NMN group and 12 in the placebo group.

  • Muscle insulin sensitivity: Insulin-stimulated glucose uptake rose by an average of 25% in the NMN group; the placebo group showed no change.
  • Cellular changes: Phosphorylation of Akt and mTOR increased in muscle tissue, along with higher expression of genes linked to muscle remodeling, including PDGFRβ.
  • No change: Liver insulin sensitivity, fasting blood glucose, blood pressure, lipid profile and inflammatory markers showed no significant difference.

The study's lead investigator said it was premature to make clinical recommendations based on these findings alone. The trial enrolled only women; the team said they were continuing a follow-up trial that includes men.

Human Data 2: Walking Speed and Grip Strength

In a 2022 trial published in npj Aging, Mitsuhiro Igarashi and colleagues randomized men over 65 to 250 mg of NMN or placebo a day for 6 or 12 weeks, in a double-blind, placebo-controlled design with 42 participants randomized in total.

  • Walking speed: Rose from 1.50 m/s to 1.60 m/s in the NMN group; the placebo group fell slightly, from 1.31 m/s to 1.30 m/s.
  • Grip strength: Left-hand grip showed a nominal improvement. Right-hand grip showed no difference.
  • Body composition: No significant change in fat or muscle mass.
  • Safety: NMN was well tolerated, with no serious adverse effects reported.

One detail worth flagging: this trial was funded by the life sciences research arm of Mitsubishi Corporation, which produces NMN raw material, and some of the authors are employees of that company. That doesn't make the findings wrong, but it is context worth keeping in mind while reading them. The authors themselves note the small sample size and call for larger studies. A later report from the same group gives the total randomized participant count as 42.

The Telomere Claim: How Solid Is It?

The most widely shared NMN claim in social media and supplement marketing is that it lengthens telomeres. It traces back to a 2021 study in Frontiers in Nutrition. One arm of that study was run in mice; the human arm involved just 8 healthy men (aged 45 to 60), who took 300 mg of NMN a day for 90 days.

It needs to be said plainly: this human arm had no placebo or control group, and the design was single-arm and unblinded. The researchers themselves state in the paper that they did not run a placebo-controlled trial. They reported a significant increase in telomere length in participants' blood cells, but without a control group there is no way to separate how much of that came from NMN versus measurement variability or a repeat-testing effect. The more dramatic summaries circulating online, claiming telomeres "nearly doubled," do not appear in the paper's own text; the exact figure is given only in a chart, and how faithfully those summaries reflect it is unclear.

An 8-person, uncontrolled pilot study is not, on its own, a basis for concluding that NMN lengthens telomeres, not without confirmation from independent, controlled trials.

Safety and Dosing

A 2022 dose-safety trial in Scientific Reports gave healthy adults up to 1,250 mg of NMN once daily for 4 weeks and found it safe and well tolerated. That is five times the 250 mg/day dose used in most of the trials above.

Still, the limit worth keeping in view: the longest published human trial ran 12 weeks. There is no data on the safety of using it for months or years. In most markets, including Turkey, NMN is sold as a dietary supplement rather than a regulated drug, which means purity and dosing can vary between manufacturers.

So Should You Take NMN?

What the evidence supports clearly: NMN raises blood NAD+ levels. Beyond that, there are two small human trials, one showing a benefit for muscle insulin sensitivity, the other for walking speed and grip in one hand. Both ran 10 to 12 weeks with 25 to 42 people, and both are described by their own authors as not yet a basis for clinical recommendations.

There isn't enough evidence to recommend NMN for slowing aging or extending lifespan. If you're considering it, choosing a brand with third-party testing, and talking to a doctor first if you're pregnant, manage a chronic condition, or take regular medication, is the sensible approach.

Limitations

  • Small samples: Every human trial involved between 8 and 42 people; none exceeds a few hundred.
  • Conflict of interest: The walking-speed trial was funded by a company that produces NMN, and some authors are its employees.
  • Short durations: The longest human trial ran 12 weeks. Long-term effect and safety are unknown.
  • No lifespan data: Neither the mouse nor the human studies show NMN extends lifespan.
  • Uncontrolled telomere data: The most shared telomere finding comes from an 8-person pilot with no placebo arm.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is NMN, and how does it relate to NAD+?

NMN (nicotinamide mononucleotide) is an intermediate molecule that cells convert into NAD+, a coenzyme involved in energy production and DNA repair. The body makes its own NMN; the idea behind supplementing is to support NAD+ levels, which fall with age. We cover NAD+ itself and its link to sirtuin genes in more depth in our NAD+ and sirtuin genes article.

Does NMN slow aging or extend lifespan?

No study shows NMN extends human lifespan. A 12-month mouse study published in 2016 found it slowed age-related physical decline, but even that study didn't measure how long the mice lived; it tracked intermediate markers like body weight, energy metabolism and insulin sensitivity. Human trials are limited to small, 10-to-12-week studies.

Does NMN improve muscle strength or fitness?

The picture is mixed. A 2021 trial in Science found NMN raised muscle insulin sensitivity in prediabetic women, with no change in blood sugar, blood pressure or cholesterol. A 2022 trial found NMN improved walking speed and left-hand grip strength in men over 65, with no change in right-hand grip or body composition. That second trial was funded by the research arm of a company that sells NMN.

How solid is the claim that NMN lengthens telomeres?

That claim comes from a 2021 pilot study in just 8 healthy men. There was no placebo or control group, and participants knew what they were taking. The researchers reported a significant increase in telomere length, but that is a single uncontrolled pilot finding that needs confirmation in independent, controlled trials.

Is NMN safe, and what dose is used?

Published human trials using 250 mg to 1,250 mg a day generally found NMN well tolerated, with no serious adverse effects reported. The longest human trial ran 12 weeks, so there is no safety data for longer-term use. In most markets NMN is sold as a supplement rather than a drug; anyone pregnant, breastfeeding, managing a chronic condition, or on regular medication should talk to a doctor before starting.
