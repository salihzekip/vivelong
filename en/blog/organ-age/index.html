Is One "Age" Even Enough?

Tools like Vivelong's biological age calculator reduce your body's overall pace of aging to a single number. But an idea that's been gaining traction in longevity science over the past few years goes further: your body may not age at a single, uniform rate — different organs may age at different speeds. In the same person, the heart might measure 45, the kidneys 58, and the immune system 63 in "biological age."

This "organ age" concept, which got broad coverage in Turkish health media in August 2026, gained concrete, long-term human data through a new preprint study published in October 2025 by researchers at University College London (UCL) and Stanford University — and the study's most striking part isn't organ age itself, but what determines it.

The Study: 15 Years Following 1,803 People Born in 1946

The study draws on 1,803 people from Britain's famous 1946 birth cohort (the MRC National Survey of Health and Development) — all born the same week. Participants gave blood samples at an average age of 63.2; using the 11k SomaScan platform, researchers measured 10,776 distinct plasma proteins to calculate a separate "proteomic age" for 7 organs/systems: the brain, heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, immune system and arteries. Participants were then followed for 15.1 years, during which 281 people (15.6%) died.

Important note: The study is currently a preprint on medRxiv and has not yet been published in a peer-reviewed journal. The findings come from a large, robust sample but haven't completed final peer review; we present them here with that caveat.

The Link to Mortality: What Do the Numbers Say?

  • Overall extreme organ aging: Mortality risk was 2.92 times higher for people with extreme accelerated aging in at least one organ, compared with those with none.
  • Extreme heart aging alone: Raised mortality risk by 2.96 times — with very high statistical significance (p ≈ 2.72×10⁻¹⁴).
  • Extreme aging in 4 or more organs: Mortality risk of 6.62 times (95% CI: 4.31–10.17) — risk compounds sharply as the number of affected organs grows.
  • Mediation analysis: Kidney aging alone explains 22.9% of how lifestyle risks translate into mortality; the liver explains 11.7%, and the immune system 9.1%.

The Real Finding: Which Lifestyle Choice Affects Which Organ

This is where the study gets genuinely practical: the authors measured, in years, the effect on organ aging of 8 different "life course exposures" spanning childhood to adulthood.

  • Being overweight in adolescence: Accelerates organ aging by an average of +1.13 years — an effect still measurable decades later.
  • Staying in education until age 16: A protective effect of −0.52 years.
  • Lifelong regular physical activity: A protective effect of −0.79 years — an independent confirmation of what we describe in our Movement & Exercise guide.
  • Smoking: +0.59 years of accelerated organ aging for every 20 pack-years.
  • Lifelong financial/psychosocial adversity: +0.24 years for every difficult life period — evidence that chronic stress leaves a mark at the organ level (see our Social Connection & Purpose guide).

The most encouraging finding: participants who had quit smoking by age 43 showed an organ-aging profile statistically indistinguishable from people who had never smoked. The group with the highest "Protective Factor Score" (a combination of factors like education, activity, non-smoking and low stress) also had a 4 times lower risk of extreme organ aging than the lowest-scoring group (OR = 0.24).

Limitations

  • Not yet peer-reviewed: These findings come from a preprint; details may change during final publication.
  • A single, homogeneous cohort: All participants were born in Britain in 1946 — how well these findings generalize to other populations (different genetic backgrounds, different environmental conditions) is a separate question.
  • Observational study: The correlations are strong, but this doesn't definitively prove that any single lifestyle factor directly causes organ aging.
  • Not clinically available: The method relies on a specialized research proteomics platform; there is no routine "organ age test" your doctor can order today.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is organ age, and how is it different from biological age?

Biological age summarizes your body's overall pace of aging as a single number. Organ age takes that idea further, proposing that each organ may have its own biological age, calculated separately from protein patterns in your blood. Within the same person, some organs can measure years younger than their chronological age, others years older.

Can I get an organ age test right now?

No, there is no routine, clinically available "organ age blood test" yet. The method used in research requires specialized platforms that measure tens of thousands of plasma proteins, plus machine learning models. The only thing measurable today is a lifestyle-based biological age estimate, like the one Vivelong offers.

Does quitting smoking actually reverse organ aging?

According to a 2026 study that followed 1,803 people for 15 years, yes, substantially: participants who had quit smoking by age 43 showed an organ-aging profile statistically indistinguishable from people who had never smoked. Because the study is observational, this isn't definitive proof of causation, but it's a strong, hopeful signal.
