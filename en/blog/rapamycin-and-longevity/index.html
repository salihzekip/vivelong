Why Did Rapamycin Become a Longevity Topic?

Rapamycin (sirolimus) has been used since 1999 to suppress the immune system after organ transplants; it was originally isolated from a soil bacterium found on Easter Island. Inside the cell, it blocks a protein called mTOR, a switch that tells cells to "grow and multiply" when nutrients are abundant. Shutting that signal off pushes the cell into recycling its own damaged parts, as if food were scarce — a process called autophagy, which we cover in more depth in our autophagy article.

Since the mid-2000s, rapamycin has stood out in mouse studies as one of the most powerful lifespan-extending pharmacological interventions tested so far. That put it at the center of the search for an "anti-aging drug" — but for years, human data was limited to observational studies and biomarker trials. That changed in 2025.

What Do the Mouse Studies Show?

The US National Institute on Aging's Interventions Testing Program (ITP) runs the same protocol across multiple independent sites, making it one of the field's most trusted sources. In mice started on rapamycin in midlife (around 20 months old), the highest dose extended median lifespan by 23% in males and 26% in females. Lower doses produced smaller effects, pointing to a dose-dependent relationship.

That result matters, but it has two limits. A mouse lifespan (2-3 years) is a small slice of a human one (70-80 years on average), and dose-response relationships don't transfer directly across species. There is no evidence that a protocol working in mice will produce the same proportional effect in people — which is exactly why human trials were needed.

PEARL: The First Large Randomized Trial in Humans

PEARL (Participatory Evaluation of Aging with Rapamycin for Longevity) was published in April 2025 in Aging (Moel, Harinath, Lee, Nyquist, Morgan, Isman and Zalzala). It was a 48-week, decentralized, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial. Healthy adults aged 50-85, aging normally, were assigned to placebo, 5 mg weekly, or 10 mg weekly of compounded rapamycin. 114 people completed the study.

The trial's primary endpoint was visceral fat, measured by DEXA — the fat compartment most tightly linked to metabolic aging and cardiovascular risk. The weekly, intermittent dosing was chosen deliberately: unlike the daily, high-dose regimen used in transplant patients, it aimed to capture the periodic benefits of mTOR inhibition while limiting immunosuppression risk.

What Did the Results Show?

  • Primary endpoint (visceral fat): No significant change in any group. The trial's main expected outcome didn't hold up.
  • Muscle mass: Women on 10 mg weekly gained 6% lean tissue mass from baseline at 48 weeks (p=0.018). Men on the same dose showed no significant change on any measure.
  • Pain and well-being: Women in the 10 mg group reported less pain. Women in the 5 mg group showed improvements in emotional well-being and general health scores.
  • Safety: Serious adverse event rates were similar across all three groups, and blood biomarkers stayed within normal range.

So the trial came back negative on the outcome everyone expected to matter most (belly fat), yet produced an unexpected positive signal in a different place — muscle and pain, and only in women. Men showed no significant difference at either dose on any measure, and the researchers don't yet know why.

What the Trial Doesn't Show

  • No lifespan data. PEARL ran for 48 weeks. We can't infer from it whether there's any meaningful counterpart in human lifespan. What was measured were intermediate markers like muscle mass and pain, not mortality or survival.
  • The finding in women is exploratory. Sex-based subgroup analysis wasn't pre-specified as a separate primary endpoint. A single trial's unplanned subgroup result needs confirmation.
  • 114 people is a small sample. Split across three arms, each group holds only a few dozen people — possibly not enough statistical power to catch real but modest effects.
  • Participants were already healthy. A result found in healthy, "normally aging" volunteers may not hold in people with chronic illness or greater frailty.

Safety and Risks

Rapamycin's safety profile comes from two different worlds, and it matters not to conflate them. In transplant patients, it's used daily for years, at doses much higher than PEARL's, to prevent organ rejection — and at those doses, known side effects include higher infection risk, mouth sores, elevated cholesterol and triglycerides, and delayed wound healing.

PEARL tested something different: a low, once-weekly, intermittent dose. Over 48 weeks, the serious adverse event rate didn't differ from placebo. That's reasonable early evidence that low, intermittent dosing looks safer than transplant-level dosing — but 48 weeks isn't enough to establish the safety of years of ongoing use. There isn't yet enough human data on long-term immune function, infection risk, or cancer screening implications.

Can You Get It Without a Prescription?

No. Rapamycin (sirolimus) is a prescription drug in Turkey, as it is in most of the world, approved for immunosuppression after organ transplants. Using it for longevity — the same use that researchers like Attia are testing in trials such as PEARL — is an off-label use that no health authority has yet approved for this purpose.

What the evidence supports so far: low, intermittent dosing looks safe in the short term, and some people — particularly women — show a measurable signal of benefit in muscle and well-being. But it didn't reduce visceral fat, produced no effect in men, and there's no human data showing it extends lifespan. For anyone curious, this isn't something to try on your own — it's a decision to work through with a doctor, ideally one experienced in this area.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does rapamycin actually extend human lifespan?

There is no evidence it extends human lifespan. In mouse studies, the highest doses extended median lifespan by 23-26%. PEARL (2025), the first large randomized human trial, found no change in belly fat after 48 weeks; women saw meaningful improvements in muscle mass and pain, while men showed no change on any measure.

How many people were in the PEARL trial?

114 healthy adults aged 50-85 completed the study. Participants were randomized to placebo, 5 mg weekly, or 10 mg weekly rapamycin, and followed for 48 weeks.

Is rapamycin safe?

In the PEARL trial, low weekly doses showed a serious adverse event rate similar to placebo, with blood markers staying within normal range. But rapamycin is also an immunosuppressant used for years at much higher daily doses in transplant patients, where infection risk, mouth sores and elevated cholesterol are known side effects. It should not be used without a prescription and medical supervision.

Can I get rapamycin for longevity purposes?

Rapamycin (sirolimus) is a prescription drug approved for preventing organ transplant rejection. Using it for longevity is an off-label use everywhere in the world, including Turkey, and should only be considered under a doctor's supervision with an individual risk assessment.
