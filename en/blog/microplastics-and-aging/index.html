Microplastics and Aging: Are Microplastics in the Brain and Arteries Harmful?

The Short Answer: Plastic Is in Our Tissues, but Harm Hasn't Been Proven

Microplastics have been measured in human blood, arterial plaque, the placenta and brain tissue, and patients with plastic detected in their plaque had more heart attacks, strokes and deaths. All of these studies are observational, which means they do not show that plastic causes disease or aging. On top of that, published work has shown that the most widely used measurement method can overstate polyethylene in fatty tissue. The honest summary today: the exposure is real, the amounts are disputed, and the health effect is uncertain.

A quick word on terms: particles between 1 micrometer and 5 millimeters are called microplastics, and those smaller than 1 micrometer are called nanoplastics. The smaller ones are thought to enter the body more easily. They are also correspondingly harder to measure, and that difficulty is the real subject of this article.

Plastic in Arterial Plaque: NEJM 2024

The study that put the topic on medicine's agenda was published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2024. In a multicenter prospective study, Marfella and colleagues enrolled 304 patients undergoing surgery for asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis and examined the plaque that was removed. Among the 257 patients who could be followed, for an average of 33.7 months, polyethylene was measured in the plaque of 150 (58.4%) and PVC as well in 31 (12.1%). Under the electron microscope, jagged-edged foreign particles were visible among the immune cells in the plaque. In patients with plastic detected in their plaque, the risk of the composite endpoint of heart attack, stroke or death from any cause was 4.53 times higher (95% confidence interval 2.00–10.27).

The number is large, but the study is observational. People with plastic in their plaque may differ in other ways too, and a study published in the European Heart Journal in 2026 offered a concrete example. Among 61 patients undergoing coronary angiography, plastic was detected in the blood of 84.2% of those who had a heart attack; the figure was 40% in those with chronic coronary disease and 31.8% in those with normal arteries. In the multivariable analysis, however, the only factor that independently predicted the presence of plastic was a history of smoking. Plastic could be a cause of the disease here, or it could be a marker that travels alongside known risks such as smoking and air pollution.

A pilot study published the same year painted a more cautious picture. Using a plastic-free sampling protocol, it examined the blood of 20 stroke patients and 15 healthy people; particles were found in 94% of participants, but the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (p = 0.21).

Microplastics in the Brain: Nature Medicine 2025

Nihart and colleagues examined liver, kidney and brain (frontal cortex) samples taken at autopsies performed in the US state of New Mexico in 2016 and 2024. In the 2024 samples, the median plastic level in liver and kidney was 433 and 404 micrograms per gram of tissue. The figure for the brain was far higher: 3,345 micrograms in the 2016 samples and 4,917 in the 2024 samples. The authors interpreted this as an increase of roughly 50% in eight years. In the brains of 12 people with a dementia diagnosis, the median reached 26,076 micrograms.

Two details got little attention in the news coverage. The authors noted that in dementia the brain tissue shrinks, the blood-brain barrier breaks down and clearance mechanisms weaken, all of which could increase plastic accumulation, and wrote that "no causality is assumed." Second, no relationship was found between plastic levels and age at death (p = 0.87). In other words, the data do not support the idea that "plastic accumulates over a lifetime and rises as we age."

The Real Debate: Were These Numbers Measured Correctly?

The main tool in both studies is pyrolysis-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (Py-GC/MS). The sample is heated and broken down in the absence of oxygen, and the molecules released are used to calculate which plastic is present and how much of it. The weak point is this: fats, when heated, also yield fragments that resemble polyethylene. The brain is a fat-rich organ, and polyethylene was the most abundant plastic in both studies.

In 2025, Rauert and colleagues in Australia put this method to the test in human blood. The extraction protocol they developed reduced the false-positive signal for polyethylene but could not eliminate it; realistic detection limits were up to 20 times those calculated with pure water. Their conclusion is clear: in its current form, Py-GC/MS is not suitable for measuring polyethylene and PVC in biological samples.

In November 2025, Nature Medicine published a critique of the brain study by Monikh and eight other researchers. The piece argues that limited contamination control and missing validation steps may affect the reliability of the reported levels. In their reply in the same issue, the study's authors acknowledged that even the best available methods carry uncertainty and discussed the concerns about the digestion method and phospholipid interference.

There are points in the authors' favor as well. The predominance of polyethylene was confirmed by infrared spectroscopy in five brain samples, the particles were imaged under the electron microscope, and five samples gave similar results in a second laboratory. They also listed lipid interference as a limitation in the paper themselves; liver and kidney tissue lost 99.4% of its mass during digestion, whereas for brain tissue the figure was only 91.8%, meaning more biological residue was left behind. The upshot is that the presence of particles in tissue is supported by more than one method, while exactly how much is there is not known. A systematic review published in 2026 arrives at the same place: 63.89% of the studies on human samples were published in 2024–2025, sample sizes are small, and preparation and reporting methods do not match one another.

Where Does the Link to Aging Come From?

From the laboratory, not from human data. A review published in 2026 summarizes how, in cell and animal experiments, plastic particles trigger cellular senescence through oxidative stress and DNA damage, with inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and cGAS-STING involved in the process. In a mouse and vascular cell study the same year, polystyrene nanoplastics thickened the aortic wall and prematurely aged the cells lining the inside of the vessels via the p53/p21 pathway. We covered the inflammatory signals secreted by senescent cells in our senolytics article, and the chronic low-grade inflammation they feed in our inflammaging article; the microplastics hypothesis proposes one more link, added to that chain from the outside.

The limit of these experiments is clear: as the authors of the brain study also point out, how the doses used in cell and animal experiments relate to people's real-world exposure is uncertain. A 2025 review in Nature Medicine writes that the early clinical findings rest on small numbers of patients and inadequate exposure measurement, and that this does not allow a sound risk assessment.

Reducing Exposure: Which Steps Have Data Behind Them?

  • Don't heat food in plastic containers: In a 2023 laboratory study from the University of Nebraska, the highest release was seen in the microwave; some containers shed 4.22 million microplastic and 2.11 billion nanoplastic particles per square centimeter in three minutes. In another study published in 2025, polypropylene storage containers released more particles in contact with 90 °C water than at room temperature. Heating food in a glass or ceramic dish is a precaution that costs nothing.
  • Bottled water: In 2024, a Columbia University team used a new imaging method to count about 240,000 plastic particles in one liter of bottled water; roughly 90% of them were nanoplastics. There is, however, no solid comparison that would let us say tap water is cleaner everywhere: in a small measurement carried out in Madeira in 2026, tap water contained an average of 1.30 microplastics per liter and bottled mineral water 0.59. That study's method can only see relatively large particles.
  • Boil hard water: In a study from Bangladesh published in 2026, boiling reduced the microplastics in drinking water by more than 70%. The effect increases with the hardness of the water, because the particles settle to the bottom together with the limescale that forms. That is why the water should be poured off after it has cooled, without stirring up what has settled.
  • Cut back on packaged and processed food: In the PERTH trial in Australia (Nature Medicine 2026), a seven-day randomized trial of 60 people, a diet with plastic contact kept to a minimum lowered urinary bisphenol A levels by 59.7%. What was measured was not plastic particles but chemicals that migrate from plastic. In a 15-person pilot study that did look at particles, no consistent difference emerged between scenarios; only plastic packaging use and ultra-processed food consumption were associated with microplastics in stool.

For none of these steps is there a study saying it "lowers disease risk." What they do is reduce, at low cost, an exposure whose harm is unproven but which has no benefit either. For the rest of your diet, see our longevity nutrition guide.

Microplastic Detox: Is There Any Evidence?

No. A study published in the Journal of Clinical Apheresis in 2026 measured microplastics in the blood before and after 174 plasma exchange procedures in 114 patients and reported that the amount in circulation fell. The authors' own note is instructive: no reduction could be seen in those with low baseline levels, because the plastic tubing set used in the procedure was releasing particles back into the blood. The study did not look at accumulation in tissues or at any health outcome. A preliminary study in 10 volunteers reporting that a supplement increased the number of particles excreted in stool does not show that the body's burden fell either. Today there is no scientific basis for undergoing plasma exchange without a medical reason or for buying a "plastic cleanse" program; if you are considering such a procedure, talk to your doctor first.

Limitations

  • Causation has not been shown: All of the vascular and brain studies are observational. Plastic may be a cause of disease, or it may be a marker of smoking, air pollution or compromised tissue barriers.
  • The measurement method is disputed: Py-GC/MS can overstate polyethylene in fatty tissue. The true amount in tissue may be lower than reported; the authors of the brain study, for their part, argue that some factors could also lead to under-measurement.
  • The studies are not independent of one another: A substantial share of the brain, placenta and testis measurements comes from the same research group, with shared authors and the same method. Independent confirmations using a different method are few.
  • The mechanistic data are non-human: The cellular senescence and inflammation findings come from cell culture and mice; the particles and doses used may not reflect everyday exposure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are microplastics really present in the human brain?

The autopsy study published in Nature Medicine in 2025 demonstrated plastic particles in brain tissue using three separate methods; under the electron microscope, most appeared as shard-like fragments 100-200 nanometers long. The presence of the particles is therefore considered plausible. What is disputed is the amount: the median of 4,917 micrograms per gram reported by the study was measured with a method that can overstate polyethylene in fatty tissue, and a critique published in the same journal questioned the reliability of the figures.

Do microplastics cause heart attacks or dementia?

No study shows that. In the NEJM 2024 study, patients with plastic detected in their carotid artery plaque had a 4.53 times higher risk of heart attack, stroke or death, but the study was observational and does not prove causation. The researchers who found higher levels in the brains of people with dementia also wrote that dementia itself may increase accumulation, which is why they do not assume causality.

Do microplastics speed up aging?

There are no human data showing this. In cell culture and mouse experiments, nanoplastics increased markers of oxidative stress, DNA damage and cellular senescence. How the doses used in those experiments relate to people's real-world exposure is unclear. In the brain autopsy study, there was also no relationship between plastic levels and age at death.

How can you reduce the microplastics that get into your body?

The steps that have data behind them target the source of exposure: not microwaving food in plastic containers, not putting hot drinks in plastic, boiling hard water and leaving the sediment behind, and cutting back on packaged, processed food. There are laboratory data showing these steps reduce particle intake; there is not yet a study showing they lower disease risk.

Does a microplastic detox or blood cleansing work?

There is no proven method. A study published in 2026 showed that plasma exchange can reduce microplastics circulating in the blood, but the procedure's plastic tubing set released particles back into the blood and no health outcome was measured. There are no human studies showing that detox products sold as supplements, teas or cleanses reduce the body's plastic burden.

"The evidence that plastic particles are present in our bodies is getting stronger. As for how much is there and what it does, we don't yet have a reliable number. Any headline written without separating the two misleads the reader."
