Taurine and Longevity: Why Was 2023's "Youth Molecule" Claim Challenged in 2025?

Science Debate · Published: September 20, 2026 · 6 min read
10-12%
Lifespan increase reported in taurine-treated mice (Science, 2023)
3 cohorts
Human data from the US, Spain and Emory (NIH / Science, 2025)
137 men

What Happened in 2023?

In 2023, a study published in Science put taurine forward as a possible driver of aging. Taurine is an amino-acid-like molecule the body makes naturally, and you also get it from meat, fish and shellfish. The researchers reported that circulating taurine falls with age in mice, monkeys and humans.

The experiments were what drew attention. When middle-aged mice were given taurine, their lifespan increased by roughly 10-12%. Improvements were also reported in markers of aging such as cellular senescence, DNA damage, mitochondrial function and low-grade inflammation. In monkeys, what was examined was healthspan-related measures, not lifespan.

The story quickly became an anti-aging headline. The paper's own assessment was more cautious, though: it said clinical studies were needed to find out whether taurine deficiency drives aging in humans.

What Changed in 2025?

In 2025, two independent studies tested the human side of the premise: does taurine really decline with age?

  • The NIH study (Science, June 5, 2025): Researchers examined plasma data from three geographically distinct human cohorts (the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging in the US, the Balearic Islands Study of Aging in Spain, and the PREMED study at Emory University), together with data from monkeys and mice. The analyses followed the same people over time as well as comparing people of different ages. Circulating taurine often increased or stayed constant with age. Within-person changes over time were frequently larger than age-related changes, and taurine levels were inconsistently associated with health measures. The team concluded that taurine is unlikely to be a good biomarker of aging.
  • The Aging Cell study (Marcangeli et al., 2025): This one was a more direct test. Serum taurine in 137 men aged 20-93 was compared with age, muscle mass, muscle strength, physical performance and mitochondrial function. Taurine was not associated with any of them. The title says it plainly: "Experimental Evidence Against Taurine Deficiency as a Driver of Aging in Humans."

Were the Mouse Results Wrong?

No, and the distinction matters. The 2025 studies do not overturn the effect of taurine supplementation on lifespan in mice. What they question is the human link in the chain: does taurine fall with age in people, and does that fall drive aging? If that link is weak, translating the mouse experiments to humans rests on thinner ground.

Science worked here the way it should. A striking finding was tested by other groups with different data, and the human side could not be confirmed in those tests. Still, the two studies don't close the debate on their own. Differences in measurement methods, samples and cohorts may have influenced the results.

Should You Take a Supplement?

These two studies don't test whether a supplement works in humans. What they test is the assumption behind "taurine falls with age, so let's supplement it." The sources we relied on for this article contain no data showing that a supplement extends human life.

Taurine occurs naturally in meat, fish and shellfish. These data give no reason for someone eating a balanced diet to start a high-dose supplement. With kidney disease, regular medication or pregnancy, the decision on a supplement should be made together with a doctor.

Limitations

  • Men only: The Aging Cell study's participants were 137 men; results may differ in women.
  • Blood level, not tissue level: The studies measured circulating taurine, which may not mirror levels inside tissues.
  • Not a supplement trial: Neither study gave people taurine and followed the outcome.
  • The mouse findings stand: The lifespan result in mice has not been refuted by these studies and could still justify human trials.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is taurine?

Taurine is an amino-acid-like compound the body makes naturally. You also get it from foods such as meat, fish and shellfish.

Does taurine slow aging?

A 2023 study in Science reported that taurine-treated mice lived roughly 10-12% longer. It has not been shown to slow aging in humans; the paper itself says clinical studies are needed.

Does taurine really decline with age?

The 2023 study reported that it does. In 2025, NIH researchers found that circulating taurine often increased or stayed constant with age across three human cohorts, and an Aging Cell study found no association between taurine and age in 137 men. The debate is not fully settled.
