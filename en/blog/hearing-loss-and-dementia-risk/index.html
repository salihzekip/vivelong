Hearing Loss and Dementia Risk: Do Hearing Aids Actually Protect You?

Why Did Hearing Loss Become a Brain Health Topic?

For a long time, hearing loss was seen as purely an ear, nose and throat issue: hearing gets harder, you get a hearing aid, that's the end of the story. Over the past fifteen years, neurologists and epidemiologists have started looking at it from another angle: is there a consistent statistical link between hearing loss and dementia, and if so, what does it actually mean?

There's plenty of coverage of this topic, but the numbers you see thrown around rarely agree with each other. One headline says risk doubles, another cites a specific percentage reduction with treatment, a third says "5 times the risk." These figures come from different studies, different comparisons and different assumptions, and lining them up without explaining what each one actually measures leaves readers with no way to tell which number to trust. This article walks through three specific, verifiable studies one at a time, and explains where those numbers come from and what they do and don't say.

The Starting Point: The 2011 Baltimore Study

It's fair to say much of this topic traces back to one study. Frank Lin's team used data from the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging to follow 639 dementia-free adults for a median of 11.9 years, publishing the results in Archives of Neurology in 2011. Over that period, 58 people developed dementia (37 of them Alzheimer's disease).

  • Mild hearing loss: dementia risk 1.89-fold vs. normal hearing (95% CI 1.00-3.58)
  • Moderate hearing loss: 3.00-fold (1.43-6.30)
  • Severe hearing loss: 4.94-fold (1.09-22.40)

The researchers also found a log-linear relationship between the severity of baseline hearing loss and dementia risk: each additional 10-decibel loss raised risk by about 27% (95% CI 1.06-1.50). The "5-fold" figure that circulates in media coverage almost certainly comes from this most-severe category — it is not a general multiplier that applies to anyone with any degree of hearing loss.

One important limitation: this is an observational study, not an experiment. It doesn't prove causation. The authors themselves flag the possibility of reverse causation: an undiagnosed, very early stage of dementia could itself impair auditory processing, meaning the association might reflect two symptoms of the same underlying process rather than hearing loss directly causing dementia. The study design can't tell these two explanations apart.

The 2024 Lancet Commission: Where Does It Fit in the Bigger Picture?

For a view that weighs the whole body of literature rather than a single study, the most comprehensive source is the Lancet Commission on dementia prevention, led by Gill Livingston and periodically updated. The 2024 update lists 14 modifiable risk factors that could reduce dementia risk if addressed (up from 12 in 2020, with vision loss and high LDL cholesterol newly added).

Under this model, midlife hearing loss is estimated to contribute to about 7% of dementia cases in the population. In the 2020 report that figure was 8.2%, making hearing loss the single largest modifiable risk factor at the time. The drop doesn't mean the evidence got weaker — the Commission explicitly states that evidence for the benefit of treating hearing loss has actually strengthened since 2020. Most of the decrease simply reflects the fact that adding two new factors redistributes the shares across the model. The combined contribution of all 14 factors also rose, from 40% in 2020 to 45% in 2024.

Do Hearing Aids Actually Protect You? The ACHIEVE Trial's Hard Answer

Observational studies show a link, but the real question is different: does treating hearing loss slow dementia? The only way to test that is a randomized controlled trial, and one has been done. Lin and colleagues' ACHIEVE trial, published in The Lancet in 2023, randomized 977 adults aged 70-84 with untreated hearing loss and no substantial cognitive impairment, recruited at four US sites, into either a hearing intervention (counseling plus hearing aids) or a health-education control.

  • Overall result: Across the full study population, 3-year cognitive decline in the hearing-intervention group was not significantly different from the control group.
  • Subgroup finding: Participants were drawn from two different sources. In the subgroup recruited from the ARIC heart study — older, with more baseline cognitive risk factors — those who received the hearing intervention showed 48% slower cognitive decline over 3 years. The difference between the two subgroups was statistically significant (interaction p=0.010).

This result needs careful reading. The 48% benefit comes from a prespecified subgroup analysis, not the trial's primary result for the whole cohort. The researchers' own interpretation is cautious: they suggest the hearing intervention may help older adults who are already at increased risk of cognitive decline, but doesn't show the same benefit in a lower-risk general population. In other words, the trial doesn't say "hearing aids prevent dementia" — it says "this may work in a specific higher-risk group, and that needs confirming."

What This Means in Practice

What the current evidence supports is this: the link between hearing loss and dementia is consistent and dose-related, but a clear causal relationship hasn't been proven yet. There's no strong evidence that hearing aids prevent dementia across the general population, but there's a finding worth taking seriously — read carefully — suggesting a possible benefit in older adults already at elevated cognitive risk.

That's still a reasonable case for not postponing a hearing test, and for viewing a hearing aid as worthwhile on its own terms rather than as a guaranteed dementia shield. Untreated hearing loss is linked to greater social isolation, communication difficulty and depression risk — all of which are independently associated with dementia in their own right. So even where the direct effect on dementia isn't firmly established, an indirect benefit through these pathways isn't far-fetched.

Dementia risk doesn't reduce to any single factor. Chronic low-grade inflammation and sleep quality are among the other modifiable areas linked to brain health. Hearing loss belongs on that list too — a factor that's still underappreciated even as the evidence behind it keeps getting stronger.

Limitations

  • Reverse causation is possible: the Baltimore study is observational; undiagnosed early dementia impairing auditory processing could partly explain the association.
  • Population-attributable-fraction estimates assume causality: the Lancet Commission's 7% figure assumes hearing loss could truly be eliminated and that doing so would prevent dementia — an assumption that's itself debated.
  • ACHIEVE's primary result was negative: the 48% benefit came from a prespecified but non-representative subgroup. It needs replication in larger, more diverse populations.
  • Follow-up windows are short: both the Baltimore study (~12 years) and ACHIEVE (3 years) use relatively short windows compared with a human lifespan.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does hearing loss increase dementia risk?

There's no single number. The 2011 Baltimore study found that, compared with normal hearing, the risk was 1.89-fold with mild hearing loss, 3.00-fold with moderate loss, and 4.94-fold (roughly 5-fold) with severe loss. These are different severity tiers from the same study, not one universal multiplier that applies to everyone with any hearing loss.

Do hearing aids prevent dementia?

It can't be said with certainty. In the 2023 ACHIEVE trial, hearing aid use did not significantly reduce 3-year cognitive decline across the full group of 977 participants. However, in a subgroup already at higher cognitive risk (the ARIC cohort), decline was 48% slower. This is a hypothesis-generating finding, not definitive proof.

Does the 2024 Lancet report still treat hearing loss as a major risk factor?

Yes. Midlife hearing loss is estimated to contribute to about 7% of dementia cases. That's down from 8.2% in the 2020 report, but most of the drop comes from the model adding two new risk factors (vision loss and high LDL cholesterol), not from weaker evidence. The Commission explicitly states that evidence for the benefit of treating hearing loss has actually strengthened since 2020.
