Strength Training and Longevity: How Many Minutes a Week Are Enough?

Movement Science · Published: October 31, 2026 · 8 min read
147,374 people
Up to 30 years of follow-up, 3 cohorts pooled (Harvard-led, BJSM, June 2026)
13-19%
Lower all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risk at 90-119 minutes of weekly strength training
~60 min/week
Weekly dose with the largest measured benefit in a 2022 meta-analysis

Why Isn't Walking Enough?

Longevity writing has long centered on cardio: walking, running, VO2 max. Strength training got filed under "for people who want muscle." But what fades with age isn't only endurance — starting in your 30s, muscle mass and strength begin a slow decline too, a process called sarcopenia. We covered how closely that loss tracks with mortality risk in our piece on grip strength.

Evidence accumulated over recent years suggests strength training belongs in the same longevity conversation as cardio. But the answer to "how much is enough" shifts depending on which study you read. Below we walk through two major studies separately and explain why they land on different numbers — collapsing them into one figure would skip half the story.

The 2026 Harvard Analysis: 30 Years of Follow-Up

An analysis led by a Harvard team, published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine in June 2026, pooled three large long-running cohort studies. A total of 147,374 adults were analyzed, roughly 79% women, with some participants followed for up to 30 years.

  • All-cause mortality: People doing 90-119 minutes of strength training a week had a 13% lower risk than those doing none.
  • Cardiovascular mortality: Risk was 19% lower in the same dose range.
  • Neurological disease mortality (such as dementia): Risk was 27% lower — the largest difference observed in the study.
  • Aerobic plus strength training combined: People who did both regular aerobic activity and 60-119 minutes of weekly strength training had up to a 45% lower mortality risk than those doing little of either. Aerobic activity alone also showed an independent link to longevity.

These figures rely on self-reported exercise, not lab measurements. And because the study is observational, people who do strength training may simply be healthier or more disciplined to begin with — factors that could be driving part of the result.

The 2022 Meta-Analysis: A Dose-Response Curve That Isn't Flat

A systematic review by Shailendra and colleagues, published in 2022 in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine, pooled 10 cohort studies of healthy adults. People doing any amount of strength training had a 15% lower all-cause mortality risk, 19% lower cardiovascular mortality risk, and 14% lower cancer mortality risk compared with those doing none.

What stands out more is the dose-response analysis. Benefit peaked at around 60 minutes a week, where all-cause mortality risk was 26% lower. A 2023 commentary on this meta-analysis (Burtscher et al., Journal of Sport and Health Science) reports that the measured benefit declined past 60 minutes, disappeared around 150 minutes a week, and in some analyses, risk appeared higher above that volume.

The authors couldn't offer a convincing biological mechanism for this and call the finding "puzzling." One possible explanation they raise is reverse causation: people who are already frail or ill may simply train less, or a small subgroup training at very high volumes may carry a different risk profile for other reasons. There's no settled answer — which means "training over 150 minutes a week is harmful" is not a conclusion this data supports.

So Do the Two Studies Contradict Each Other?

No — they complement each other. The Harvard analysis compares one specific dose range (90-119 minutes) against doing none, and finds a consistent benefit in that range. The 2022 meta-analysis draws a more detailed dose-response curve, showing the peak benefit may sit at a lower dose (around 60 minutes), with the curve flattening or reversing at much higher volumes. Both rely on different cohorts, different measurement methods, and different statistical models — some divergence in the numbers is expected.

The common thread: going from zero to something makes the biggest difference, somewhere between one and two hours a week looks like the most consistent benefit zone across studies, and there's no solid evidence that more is always better. The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines reflect this too — adults are advised to do muscle-strengthening activity involving major muscle groups at least 2 days a week, alongside 150-300 minutes of moderate (or 75-150 minutes of vigorous) aerobic activity, without specifying an exact minute target.

What Should You Actually Do?

Free weights, machines, resistance bands, or bodyweight movements like push-ups and squats — when studies say "strength" or "resistance" training, they generally mean all of these, not one specific method. What matters is loading the major muscle groups (legs, back, chest, core) on a regular basis.

Targeting at least 2 days a week, totaling somewhere between one and two hours, lines up best with the current evidence. If you're doing nothing now, starting with two short sessions a week will do more than searching for the "optimal" program. We've also covered, in a separate article, that adding creatine to a regular strength routine may give a small boost to strength gains in people over 55 — though it's a supplement, not a prerequisite.

If you have heart disease, high blood pressure, joint issues, or are pregnant, it's safer to plan intensity and exercise selection with a doctor or exercise professional.

Limitations

  • Observational data: No study randomly assigned people to strength training and measured mortality — that would be an extremely difficult trial to run ethically. Everything here is associational.
  • Self-reported exercise: Participants reported their own training, which is prone to error.
  • Little high-dose data: People training more than 150 minutes a week are a small minority in these studies, making estimates in that range less reliable.
  • Different populations: These studies were run in different countries, age groups, and health profiles; results may not apply equally to every individual.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does strength training extend lifespan?

No study proves strength training directly extends human lifespan. Observational studies show lower all-cause mortality risk among people who train regularly; a 2026 analysis following 147,374 people for up to 30 years found a 13% lower risk among those doing 90-119 minutes of strength training a week. That is an association, not proof of causation.

How many minutes of strength training a week are enough?

The World Health Organization recommends muscle-strengthening activity involving major muscle groups on at least 2 days a week for adults. A 2022 meta-analysis found the largest benefit at around 60 minutes a week; the 2026 Harvard-led analysis looked at the 90-119 minute range. Both point to roughly 1-2 hours a week being beneficial, without full agreement on one exact number.

Can too much strength training be harmful?

A 2023 commentary interpreting the dose-response data from the 2022 meta-analysis reports that the measured benefit peaked around 60 minutes a week, disappeared around 150 minutes, and in some analyses risk appeared to rise above that. The authors could not offer a clear biological mechanism and stressed this is not a reason for healthy people to cut back on strength training. Since the data is observational, reverse causation is also possible.

"Going from zero to something makes the biggest difference. Somewhere between one and two hours a week is the sweet spot the current evidence points to — but it's too early to say more is always better."
